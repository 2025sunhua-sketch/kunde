What a Product Page Tells You, and What Only a Datasheet Can

  1. Home
  2. Knowledge
  3. What a Product Page Tells You, and What Only a Datasheet Can
2026-11-10 3 min read Kunde Electric Engineering Team

A practical reading of product titles, photographs and structured data on this catalogue, plus the information to include when you request written confirmation.

On this page

  1. What you can read directly
  2. Why there is no quotation, threshold or dispatch window
  3. Dimensions live inside the photographs
  4. What to include in an enquiry
  5. Reading a page as evidence

A product page in this catalogue is a faithful presentation of the source listing: the title and the photographs are reproduced as supplied, and the page adds navigation, structured data and a way to get in touch. It is not a datasheet. Knowing exactly which facts are on the page, and which are deliberately absent, saves a round trip when you are building a shortlist for a project.

What you can read directly

Each page carries one product title, one product reference, a main photograph and the detail photographs that belong to that listing. The breadcrumb shows the category family, and the related products block points to other items in the same family. The page also carries machine-readable structured data describing the product, its name and its image, so that search engines and answer engines can identify the page correctly.

The product reference is stable. It appears in the page address and as the product identifier in the structured data, and it does not change when a category is renamed or reordered. If you quote that reference in an email, the reply will be about the same page you were looking at, even months later.

Why there is no quotation, threshold or dispatch window

Product pages do not show a quotation, an order threshold, a dispatch window, a production capacity, a headcount, a service-country count, an age of business, or a response-time promise. They also do not display a badge or a reference to a named compliance document. Those statements are simply not published here, because a catalogue page is not the place to assert something that has not been confirmed in writing for the exact part you are buying.

The same restraint applies to the structured data. Product markup deliberately contains no offer and no rating, so a machine reading the page cannot invent commercial terms that are not there. This is a design decision, not a missing feature: it keeps the page honest for both people and automated readers.

Dimensions live inside the photographs

For many items in this range, the dimensional information exists only as a table drawn into one of the detail photographs. The page does not retype those numbers into an HTML table, because transcribing a drawing by hand is exactly how a wrong value ends up in a specification. Open the detail photograph, read the table in the image at full size, and treat it as a reference to be confirmed rather than as a controlled document.

If your drawing package needs a value in text form, ask for it in writing against the product reference. That gives you a version you can file with the rest of the project documentation.

What to include in an enquiry

  • The exact product reference, or a short list of references if you are comparing options.
  • Conductor material and size, and whether the joint is copper-to-copper, aluminum-to-aluminum or copper-to-aluminum.
  • The application and environment: indoor or outdoor, fixed installation or equipment wiring, exposure to moisture or salt air, and the type of enclosure.
  • The termination format you need: bare, insulated, block, clamp or a specific mounting style.
  • Any drawing, photograph or written requirement that the reply must address, and the document you need in return.

Quantity is useful context for a conversation, but the site does not publish an order threshold, so there is no number to guess at before you write. What matters most is that the request names a real page and describes the joint you are trying to make.

Reading a page as evidence

Treat the page as a way to identify an item and the photographs as a way to recognise it. Treat written confirmation as the source for anything that will appear in a specification, a purchase document or a calculation. That split is the whole reason the commercial and compliance fields are empty here, and it is also what makes a shortlist built from these pages reliable to share with colleagues.
