Copper, Aluminum or Bimetal: Choosing a Termination Family

  1. Home
  2. Knowledge
  3. Copper, Aluminum or Bimetal: Choosing a Termination Family
2026-11-10 3 min read Kunde Electric Engineering Team

A qualitative guide to copper, aluminum and bimetal termination families, and the installation details that belong in a written datasheet rather than a catalogue photo.

On this page

  1. Why the conductor material comes first
  2. Where each family is commonly used
  3. Format matters as much as material
  4. Details that belong in a written datasheet
  5. A short checklist before you write

Choosing a termination is mostly a question of which conductor arrives at the joint and what it is being joined to. This catalogue separates those decisions into visible families so that you can reach the right shortlist quickly. The families here are descriptive, not prescriptive: they tell you what the part is, while the written documents you request later tell you whether it suits your installation.

Why the conductor material comes first

Copper and aluminum behave differently at a joint. Aluminum forms a surface oxide quickly, is softer under a clamping load and moves more than copper as temperature changes. Copper is denser, stiffer and commonly used for busbars and equipment terminals. When a copper conductor meets an aluminum one, the pair also forms a dissimilar-metal couple, which is why the interface deserves more attention than a simple lap joint.

None of that means one material is universally better. It means the termination has to be chosen for the pair of surfaces actually present. A part that works well on a copper-to-copper joint is not automatically the right answer for an aluminum conductor landing on a copper busbar.

Where each family is commonly used

  • Copper Lugs, 33 products. Copper conductors terminating at copper equipment or busbars. This is the family most buyers start with when both sides of the joint are copper.
  • Aluminum Lugs, 12 products. Aluminum conductors where the receiving side is compatible with aluminum, and the joint detail is confirmed in writing.
  • Bimetal Lugs, 36 products. The transition family, used where an aluminum conductor meets a copper termination. The copper-bearing and aluminum-bearing portions are joined in the part itself so that the dissimilar-metal interface is not left to the installation.
  • Bare Terminals, 16 products, and Insulated Terminals, 69 products. The same decision expressed as a format: an uninsulated body for a protected enclosure, or a sleeved body where added insulation at the termination is wanted.
  • Terminal Blocks, 72 products, and New Energy Terminals, 14 products. Distribution and equipment-side connection formats, chosen by how the conductors are arranged rather than by a single lug shape.

The counts are the number of products listed in each family on this site. They are a navigation aid, not a stock statement and not a recommendation.

Format matters as much as material

Once the material pair is settled, the mechanical format decides whether the part can actually be installed. A crimp body needs a matching tool and die, a bolted or clamped body needs a defined tightening sequence, and an insulated sleeve needs enough room in the enclosure. These are installation details, not catalogue descriptions, and they are the reason two visually similar parts can behave differently in service.

The detail photographs on a product page are useful for recognising a shape, a barrel length, a pad hole pattern or a sleeve colour. Where a dimensional table appears in a photograph, read it at full size and then ask for the same information in writing before using it in a drawing.

Details that belong in a written datasheet

  • Conductor size range and the exact body or die reference for the installation tool.
  • Material and plating of each part of the body, and how the transition between metals is made.
  • Tightening instructions, the number of crimps, and the inspection criteria for a finished joint.
  • Operating temperature range, environment limits and any documentation you need to file with the project.

These are precisely the values that are not typed into catalogue pages here. A photograph cannot be a controlled source for a torque value, and a title cannot tell you which die to use. Ask for the datasheet against the product reference, and keep it with the rest of the project records.

A short checklist before you write

Name the conductor on each side of the joint, choose the family that matches the pair, pick the format that fits the enclosure, then shortlist two or three product references and read their detail photographs. Send that shortlist with your application description and a list of the values you need confirmed. A reply tied to a specific reference is far more useful than a general discussion about copper versus aluminum.
